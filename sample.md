# SALIENT FEATURES OF THE CONSTITUTION

## Introductory Points
- Nature and Evolution of the Constitution
  - Indian Constitution is unique in its content and spirit[cite: 3]
  - Though borrowed from many Constitutions, it has highly distinctive features[cite: 3]
  - Several original features of 1949 have been substantially changed through amendments (including 7th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 97th, and 101st Amendments)[cite: 3]
  - 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 is widely known as the "Mini-Constitution" due to its massive changes[cite: 3]
  - Basic Structure Limitation (Kesavananda Bharati Case, 1973): Supreme Court ruled that Parliament's constituent power under Article 368 cannot be used to alter or destroy the basic structure of the Constitution[cite: 3]
- Current Numerical Framework (as of 2019)
  - Contains a Preamble, ~470 Articles divided into 25 Parts, and 12 Schedules[cite: 3]
  - Cumulative Changes:
    - Deleted: 20 Articles and 1 Part (Part VII)[cite: 3]
    - Added: ~95 Articles, 4 Parts (IVA, IXA, IXB, and XIV-A), and 4 Schedules (9, 10, 11, and 12)[cite: 3]
  - Holds the distinction where no other Constitution in the world contains so many articles and schedules[cite: 3]
- Factors Contributing to Elephantine Length
  - Geographical Factors: India's vastness and diversity[cite: 3]
  - Historical Factors: The massive influence of the Government of India Act, 1935 (which was itself very bulky)[cite: 3]
  - Political Factors: Having a single Constitution for both the Centre and the States (except erstwhile J&K)[cite: 3]
  - Constituent Assembly Composition: The absolute dominance of legal experts in the Assembly[cite: 3]
  - Content Structure: Inclusion of detailed administrative details alongside fundamental principles, which are left to conventions in other nations[cite: 3]

## Salient Features of the Constitution
- 1. Lengthiest Written Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Constitutions are classified into Written (like the USA) and Unwritten (like Britain)[cite: 3]
  - The Indian Constitution is the longest written Constitution in the world[cite: 3]
  - Original Structure (1949): Contained a Preamble, 395 Articles (grouped in 22 Parts), and 8 Schedules[cite: 3]
- 2. Drawn from Various Sources[cite: 3]
  - Framed after "ransacking all known Constitutions" (Dr. B.R. Ambedkar)[cite: 3]
  - Structural Part: Derived heavily from the Government of India Act, 1935[cite: 3]
  - Philosophical Part:
    - Fundamental Rights: Inspired by the American Constitution[cite: 3]
    - Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs): Inspired by the Irish Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Political Part: The principles of Cabinet Government and executive-legislature relations are derived from the British Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Other Sources: Canada, Australia, Germany, USSR, France, South Africa, and Japan[cite: 3]
  - Influence of GOI Act 1935: More than half of the provisions are identical or close to the 1935 Act, including the Federal Scheme, Judiciary, Governors, Emergency Powers, Public Service Commissions, and administrative details[cite: 3]
- 3. Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility[cite: 3]
  - Rigid Constitution: Requires a special procedure for its amendment (e.g., USA)[cite: 3]
  - Flexible Constitution: Can be amended in the same manner as ordinary laws (e.g., Britain)[cite: 3]
  - Indian Synthesis: Neither rigid nor flexible, but a synthesis of both[cite: 3]
  - Article 368 Amendment Channels:
    - Special Majority: Passed by 2/3rd of members present and voting AND a majority of the total membership of each House[cite: 3]
    - Special Majority + State Ratification: Special majority in Parliament plus ratification by at least half of the state legislatures[cite: 3]
  - Simple Majority Amendments: Certain provisions can be amended by a simple majority of Parliament; these explicitly do not fall under Article 368[cite: 3]
- 4. Federal System with Unitary Bias[cite: 3]
  - Federal Features: Two governments, division of powers, written Constitution, supremacy of the Constitution, rigidity, independent judiciary, and bicameralism[cite: 3]
  - Unitary/Non-Federal Features: Strong Centre, single Constitution, single citizenship, flexibility of the Constitution, integrated judiciary, Governor appointed by the Centre, All-India Services, and emergency provisions[cite: 3]
  - "Federation" Word Usage: The term "Federation" is nowhere used in the Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Article 1 Designation: Describes India as a "Union of States"[cite: 3]
    - Implications: The Indian Federation is not the result of an agreement by states, and no state has the right to secede from the Union[cite: 3]
  - Scholarly Classifications:
    - Quasi-federal: Termed by K.C. Wheare[cite: 3]
    - Bargaining federalism: Termed by Morris Jones[cite: 3]
    - Cooperative federalism: Termed by Granville Austin[cite: 3]
    - Federation with a centralising tendency: Termed by Sir Ivor Jennings[cite: 3]
- 5. Parliamentary Form of Government[cite: 3]
  - Adopted the British Parliamentary System rather than the American Presidential System[cite: 3]
  - Emphasizes cooperation and co-ordination between the legislative and executive organs[cite: 3]
  - Also referred to as the Westminster Model, Responsible Government, and Cabinet Government[cite: 3]
  - Exists at both the Centre and the States[cite: 3]
  - Key Features:
    - Presence of nominal and real executives[cite: 3]
    - Majority party rule[cite: 3]
    - Collective responsibility of the executive to the legislature[cite: 3]
    - Double Membership: Ministers are mandatory members of the legislature[cite: 3]
    - Leadership of the Prime Minister at the Centre and Chief Minister in States[cite: 3]
    - Dissolution of the lower house (Lok Sabha / Legislative Assembly)[cite: 3]
  - Differences from the British Model:
    - The Indian Parliament is not a sovereign body (unlike the British Parliament)[cite: 3]
    - India has an elected head of state (republican President), while Britain has a hereditary monarch[cite: 3]
  - Dominance of PM: Due to the immense power of the Prime Minister, it is often termed "Prime Ministerial Government"[cite: 3]
- 6. Synthesis of Parliamentary Sovereignty & Judicial Supremacy[cite: 3]
  - Synthesis of British Parliamentary Sovereignty and American Judicial Supremacy[cite: 3]
  - Scope of Judicial Review: Narrower in India compared to the USA[cite: 3]
    - The US Constitution is based on "due process of law"[cite: 3]
    - The Indian Constitution is based on "procedure established by law" under Article 21[cite: 3]
  - Balanced Scheme:
    - The Supreme Court can declare parliamentary laws unconstitutional through judicial review[cite: 3]
    - Parliament can amend the major parts of the Constitution through its constituent power under Article 368[cite: 3]
- 7. Integrated and Independent Judiciary[cite: 3]
  - Integrated Hierarchy: Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts at the state level, and subordinate courts (district and lower courts) below them[cite: 3]
  - Single Enforcement System: A single system of courts enforces both Central and State laws, unlike the USA where federal laws are enforced by federal courts and state laws by state courts[cite: 3]
  - Supreme Court Status: Serves as the federal court, the highest court of appeal, the guarantor of Fundamental Rights, and the guardian of the Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Judicial Independence Safeguards:
    - Security of tenure for judges[cite: 3]
    - Fixed service conditions[cite: 3]
    - Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India[cite: 3]
    - Ban on discussion of judges' conduct in legislatures[cite: 3]
    - Ban on practice after retirement[cite: 3]
    - Power to punish for contempt of court[cite: 3]
    - Separation of judiciary from the executive under Article 50[cite: 3]
- 8. Fundamental Rights[cite: 3]
  - Enshrined in Part III[cite: 3]
  - Six Fundamental Rights (originally seven; the Right to Property [Article 31] was repealed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978):[cite: 3]
    - Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)[cite: 3]
    - Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)[cite: 3]
    - Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)[cite: 3]
    - Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)[cite: 3]
    - Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)[cite: 3]
    - Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)[cite: 3]
  - Core Objectives: To promote political democracy and prevent authoritarian or despotic rule[cite: 3]
  - Character: They are justiciable and directly enforceable by courts; an aggrieved person can approach the SC to issue prerogative writs[cite: 3]
  - Qualified nature: Not absolute; subject to reasonable restrictions and can be amended or temporarily suspended during a National Emergency (except Articles 20 and 21)[cite: 3]
- 9. Directive Principles of State Policy[cite: 3]
  - Located in Part IV of the Constitution; described by Ambedkar as a "novel feature"[cite: 3]
  - Classified into three broad categories: Socialistic, Gandhian, and Liberal-Intellectual[cite: 3]
  - Objectives: Aimed at establishing a welfare state and promoting social and economic democracy[cite: 3]
  - Nature: Non-justiciable; they are moral obligations on the State[cite: 3]
  - Minerva Mills Case (1980) Doctrine: The Constitution is built on the bedrock of the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles[cite: 3]
- 10. Fundamental Duties[cite: 3]
  - Not present in the original Constitution; added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 based on the Swaran Singh Committee recommendations[cite: 3]
  - 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added one more duty[cite: 3]
  - Structure: Enshrined in Part IVA under Article 51A, containing 11 duties[cite: 3]
  - Nature: Non-justiciable civic and moral duties inspired by the USSR Constitution[cite: 3]
- 11. A Secular State[cite: 3]
  - The Constitution does not uphold any particular religion as the official state religion[cite: 3]
  - Secular word was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976[cite: 3]
  - Key Secular Provisions:
    - Preamble guarantees liberty of belief, faith, and worship[cite: 3]
    - Article 14: Equality before the law for all[cite: 3]
    - Article 15: No discrimination on grounds of religion[cite: 3]
    - Article 16: Equal opportunity in public employment[cite: 3]
    - Article 25: Freedom of conscience, profession, practice, and propagation of religion[cite: 3]
    - Article 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs[cite: 3]
    - Article 27: No taxation for the promotion of any particular religion[cite: 3]
    - Article 28: No religious instruction in educational institutions wholly maintained by State funds[cite: 3]
    - Articles 29 & 30: Protection of language, script, culture, and minority educational institutions[cite: 3]
    - Article 44: State shall endeavour to secure a Uniform Civil Code[cite: 3]
  - Indian Concept of Secularism: A positive concept giving equal respect and state support to all religions, unlike the Western negative concept of complete separation[cite: 3]
  - Representation: Communal electorates were abolished, but reservations for SC/ST continue[cite: 3]
- 12. Universal Adult Franchise[cite: 3]
  - Adopted as the basis of elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies[cite: 3]
  - Voting Age: Reduced from 21 to 18 years by the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988[cite: 3]
  - Non-discrimination: Every citizen of age has the right to vote without discrimination based on caste, race, religion, sex, literacy, or wealth[cite: 3]
- 13. Single Citizenship[cite: 3]
  - India has only single citizenship (Indian), unlike the USA/Switzerland where dual citizenship exists (national + state)[cite: 3]
  - Guarantees equal rights across the entire country, though communal, caste, and linguistic conflicts still occur[cite: 3]
- 14. Independent Bodies[cite: 3]
  - Establishes several independent bodies as bulwarks of the democratic system:[cite: 3]
    - Election Commission: Ensures free and fair elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and offices of President and Vice-President[cite: 3]
    - Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG): Audits Union and State accounts; acts as the guardian of the public purse[cite: 3]
    - Union Public Service Commission (UPSC): Conducts examinations for recruitment to All-India and Central services; advises the President on disciplinary matters[cite: 3]
    - State Public Service Commission (SPSC): Conducts recruitment to state services; advises the Governor[cite: 3]
  - Independence Safeguards: Ensured by security of tenure, fixed service conditions, and expenses charged directly on the Consolidated Fund[cite: 3]
- 15. Emergency Provisions[cite: 3]
  - Enables the President to meet abnormal situations effectively[cite: 3]
  - Three Types of Emergencies:
    - National Emergency (Article 352): Imposed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion[cite: 3]
    - President’s Rule / State Emergency (Articles 356 & 365): Imposed due to failure of constitutional machinery or failure to comply with Centre's directions[cite: 3]
    - Financial Emergency (Article 360): Imposed due to threat to the financial stability or credit of India[cite: 3]
  - Structural Transformation: Converts the federal polity into a unitary one without a formal constitutional amendment[cite: 3]
- 16. Three-tier Government[cite: 3]
  - Originally provided a dual polity (Centre and States)[cite: 3]
  - Added a third tier of local government through the 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts, 1992[cite: 3]
  - Panchayats (73rd Amendment): Constitutionalized rural local bodies by adding Part IX and Schedule 11[cite: 3]
  - Municipalities (74th Amendment): Constitutionalized urban local bodies by adding Part IXA and Schedule 12[cite: 3]
- 17. Co-operative Societies[cite: 3]
  - Added by the 97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011[cite: 3]
  - Three Major Changes:
    - Right to form co-operative societies made a Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(c)[cite: 3]
    - Added DPSP Article 43B for the promotion of co-operative societies[cite: 3]
    - Inserted a new Part IXB (Articles 243ZH to 243ZT) to ensure democratic, autonomous, and professional functioning[cite: 3]

## Criticism of the Constitution
- 1. Borrowed Constitution / Bag of Borrowings[cite: 3]
  - Criticised as a "patchwork" and "bag of borrowings"[cite: 3]
  - Ambedkar's Defense: A written constitution created late in history is bound to have similarities; the provisions were modified to suit Indian conditions and avoid blind copying[cite: 3]
- 2. Carbon Copy of the 1935 Act[cite: 3]
  - Critics claimed it reproduced a massive number of provisions from the Government of India Act, 1935[cite: 3]
  - Observations:
    - N. Srinivasan: The Constitution is "both in language and substance a close copy of the Act of 1935"[cite: 3]
    - Sir Ivor Jennings: It derives directly from the 1935 Act, copying many provisions almost textually[cite: 3]
    - P.R. Deshmukh: It is essentially the 1935 Act with adult franchise added[cite: 3]
  - Ambedkar's Response: There is no shame in borrowing, and no one holds a patent over constitutional ideas; the copied parts mostly relate to detailed administrative machinery[cite: 3]
- 3. Un-Indian or Anti-Indian[cite: 3]
  - Criticised as unsuitable and unworkable for India because it does not reflect local traditions[cite: 3]
  - Key Statements:
    - K. Hanumanthaiya: "We wanted the music of Veena or Sitar, but here we have the music of an English band."[cite: 3]
    - Lokanath Misra: Described the Constitution as a "slavish imitation of the West"[cite: 3]
- 4. An Un-Gandhian Constitution[cite: 3]
  - Criticised for not incorporating the ideals of Mahatma Gandhi, particularly the structure of village and district panchayats[cite: 3]
  - K. Hanumanthaiya: Clarified that this is exactly the kind of Constitution Gandhi did not want or envisage[cite: 3]
  - T. Prakasam: Attributed this lack of Gandhian philosophy to Ambedkar's non-participation in the Gandhian movement and antagonism toward his ideas[cite: 3]
- 5. Elephantine Size[cite: 3]
  - Criticised as too bulky and detailed[cite: 3]
  - Sir Ivor Jennings: Noted that the borrowed provisions were not always well-selected and that the document is far too long[cite: 3]
  - H.V. Kamath: Pointed out that since the emblem of the Assembly was an elephant, it is natural that the Constitution is the bulkiest the world has produced[cite: 3]
- 6. Paradise of the Lawyers[cite: 3]
  - Criticised as too legalistic and drafted in highly complex legal language[cite: 3]
  - Sir Ivor Jennings: Famously coined the term "lawyer's paradise"[cite: 3]
  - H.K. Maheswari: Claimed the draft would make people more litigious and open up endless avenues for court cases[cite: 3]
  - P.R. Deshmukh: Noted that the text contains too much padding and verbiage, when it should have been a simple socio-political document[cite: 3]

## TABLE 3.3 – Schedules of the Constitution
- First to Sixth Schedules[cite: 3]
  - First Schedule: Contains names of States & UTs with their territorial extent; linked to Articles 1 and 4[cite: 3]
  - Second Schedule: Contains salaries, allowances, and privileges of:[cite: 3]
    - The President of India and Governors of States[cite: 3]
    - Speakers and Deputy Speakers of Lok Sabha & State Assemblies[cite: 3]
    - Chairmen and Deputy Chairmen of Rajya Sabha & State Legislative Councils[cite: 3]
    - Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts[cite: 3]
    - Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (CAG)[cite: 3]
    - Linked to Articles 59, 65, 75, 97, 125, 148, 158, 164, 186, and 221[cite: 3]

  - Fourth Schedule: Deals with allocation of seats in the Rajya Sabha to States and UTs; linked to Articles 4 and 80[cite: 3]
  - Fifth Schedule: Deals with provisions relating to the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes; linked to Article 244[cite: 3]
  - Sixth Schedule: Provisions relating to the administration of tribal areas in the states of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram; linked to Articles 244 and 275[cite: 3]
- Seventh to Twelfth Schedules[cite: 3]
  - Seventh Schedule: Divides powers into three legislative lists (linked to Article 246):[cite: 3]

## TABLE 3.4 – Sources of the Constitution & Borrowed Features
- Domestic and British Sources[cite: 3]
  - Government of India Act, 1935: Federal scheme, office of Governor, judiciary, Public Service Commissions, Emergency Powers, and administrative details[cite: 3]
  - British Constitution: Parliamentary system, Rule of Law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, Cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges, and bicameralism[cite: 3]
- American, Irish, and Canadian Sources[cite: 3]
  - US Constitution: Fundamental Rights, independence of judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, and the post of Vice-President[cite: 3]
  - Irish Constitution: Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs), nomination of members to Rajya Sabha, and method of election of the President[cite: 3]
  - Canadian Constitution: Federation with a strong Centre, vesting of residuary powers in the Centre, appointment of state Governors by the Centre, and advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court[cite: 3]
- Continental, Soviet, and Hemispheric Sources[cite: 3]
  - Australian Constitution: Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce, and intercourse, and joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament[cite: 3]
  - Weimar Constitution of Germany: Suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency[cite: 3]
  - Soviet Constitution (USSR / Russia): Fundamental Duties and the ideals of justice (social, economic, and political) in the Preamble[cite: 3]
  - French Constitution: Republic and the ideals of liberty, equality, and fraternity in the Preamble[cite: 3]
  - South African Constitution: Procedure for amendment of the Constitution and election of members of Rajya Sabha[cite: 3]
  - Japanese Constitution: Concept of "procedure established by law"[cite: 3]
